The Bar Mitzvah party I gave up for an Intel 386.

No regrets. Just DOS, a 40 MB hard drive, and a lot of trial and error.

Small specifications. A very large world to explore.

When my family asked how I wanted to celebrate my Bar Mitzvah, I gave up the celebration so my parents could use the money to buy my first computer.

It was an IBM-compatible Intel 386. I installed DOS and began experimenting with commands while barely knowing English. Sometimes I understood what I was doing. Sometimes the computer became less enthusiastic about starting.

Then I installed everything again. That machine taught me how I still learn today: explore, experiment, break something occasionally, understand why it broke, and make the next version better.

AGE17

The unofficial neighborhood help desk

By seventeen, I could assemble computers from individual components, replace memory and hard drives, install operating systems, and diagnose hardware and software problems. Friends, relatives, and neighbors started bringing me their computers.

One challenge involved splitting a 5 MB game across several 1.2 MB floppy disks and making it run on another machine. There was no cloud storage, no USB drive, and no convenient Share button. Just floppy disks, patience, and the suspicion that there had to be a way.

02

THE SLIGHTLY INDIRECT ROUTE

CAREER JOURNEY

IT to QA to automation to development.

It was not a sudden career change. Each chapter gave me the questions, habits, and tools for the next one.

01
Hardware, networks, infrastructure, and real users

IT and systems

A childhood friend introduced me to professional computer work. I started by helping anyone who needed it for free, then became a technician, an IT manager for a hotel chain in Romania, and later managed networks for a European shipping company.

The system is always bigger than the computer.
02
Chip-PC, virtual environments, and a job ad that made no sense

Quality assurance

The role asked for an experienced IT person to become a software tester. I applied mostly because I wanted to understand why. The product served network administrators, so knowing the users and their environment mattered as much as knowing how to test.

Years of breaking my own computer turned out to be useful training.
03
SolarEdge, 2015 to 2018

Automation engineering

After manual QA, I wanted code to handle the repetitive work. Automation quickly expanded into Java frameworks, APIs, databases, Docker environments, Jenkins, CI/CD, and technical foundations that other engineers could build on.

Automation was not the destination. It was the doorway.
04
ZoomInfo, 2018 to 2020

Building the automation practice

Together with my counterpart in the United States, I helped establish the company-wide automation infrastructure from the ground up. After a successful pilot, I recruited and trained approximately nine engineers in Israel and worked alongside them during their first months.

Once the system could run without me, I knew it was time for my next challenge.
05
ZoomInfo, 2020 to today

Product development

I asked to move into development, completed the internal technical assessment, passed it, and joined a product team. It made official what I had already been doing after hours for years: building software people actually use.

The title finally caught up with the habit.

BEFORE “DEVELOPER” WAS THE TITLE

REAL PEOPLE, REAL NEEDS

I was building products before I was hired to build products.

My usual response to an annoying process was not another spreadsheet. It was to start building.

01

For our building committee

Resident announcements, issue tracking, suppliers, and expenses in one practical system.

Used in real life, then replaced as needs and technology moved on.
02

For a judo club

Student registration, attendance, lessons, and communication with parents.

Built for a friend, not for a hypothetical portfolio persona.
03

For a beauty business

Customer management, appointments, and treatment history without unnecessary complexity.

A small product shaped around one real business and its workflow.

These systems entered regular use and were eventually replaced as needs and technology evolved. What stayed with me was the experience of listening, translating everyday frustration into a product, and supporting software after someone begins depending on it.

05

LIDA SOFTWARE

THE SIDE PROJECT THAT FORGOT TO STAY SMALL

Useful tools, built from real annoyances and shared freely.

LiDa did not begin with a business plan. It did not even begin with a plan for a website.

I built a few tools for myself because I could not find solutions that worked exactly the way I needed. They were useful, so I shared them. Then I built another. And another.

Everything is free. It is also how I learn: when I want to understand a technology or product idea, I build something with it.

if (friction.repeats()) buildTool().shareFree();
LiDa

Named after Lia and Daniel. Built with curiosity. Shared without a paywall.

OUTSIDE THE TERMINAL

TRAIN / LEARN / REPEATFeedback is rather immediate here.

DISCIPLINE BEYOND SOFTWARE

Patience, fundamentals, and staying calm under pressure.

I train regularly in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu and Krav Maga, hold a black belt in Karate, and maintain a consistent gym routine.

Martial arts and engineering have more in common than they first appear. Both involve repetition, attention to fundamentals, learning from failure, and accepting that something will eventually expose a weakness in your approach.

In martial arts, that feedback is immediate. In software, it usually arrives through an alert at an inconvenient time.

THE PEOPLE BEHIND THE NAME

Good work rarely happens alone.

My wife works in graphic design and teaches design-related subjects. She gives me honest visual feedback, supports the time I spend building, and encourages me to keep learning.

“Honest visual feedback” occasionally means that something I considered finished is, in fact, not finished. She is usually right.

My children, Lia and Daniel, are the source of the LiDa name and a large part of the motivation behind the project.
